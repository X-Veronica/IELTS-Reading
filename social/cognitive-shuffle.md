# Do the Cognitive Shuffle: A Trick to Sedate Your Synapses
**Source**: BBC Science Focus | **Topic**: Psychology & Sleep Science
**Difficulty**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Targeting IELTS Band 6.0-7.0)

---

## 📖 Article Content

The human brain is a remarkable lump of electrified glob—the most complex object in the known Universe, or so we're told. But every now and again, especially at night, don't you wish it would just shut up? Racing thoughts are one of the most common symptoms when we can't sleep. The lights go out, our heads hit the pillow, but our frontal cortex isn't done for the day. Egged on by caffeine, anxiety or everyday stress, our brains start acting like a pinball machine as thoughts ricochet from one place to another. 

Psychologists call it **rumination** or mental disturbance. It's a repetitive pattern of negative thinking, and some of it is not even conscious. We dwell on mistakes, over-analyse the day, and worry about tomorrow. It's worse in people with mental health conditions, but it can happen to anyone, especially when there's too much to do.

Psychologist Dr. Luc Beaudoin believes he has a solution. It's called the **'cognitive shuffle'**—a mind trick that exploits what happens in the brain during sleep onset, that dream-like in-between land where you're not quite conscious, but not quite asleep. "Sleep onset isn't instantaneous. It proceeds gradually," explains Beaudoin, an adjunct professor at Simon Fraser University.

The cognitive shuffle aims to bring some light structure to your thoughts. It mimics what the brain does naturally, producing something like a picture show of unrelated imagery that lulls you unconscious. "It basically gets you to imagine things one after another," Beaudoin says. For example, pick a word like 'piano'. Imagine a piano for 5-6 seconds. Then, spell the word and for each letter, come up with as many associations as you can. For 'P', you might think of *peach, Pluto, pupil, pumpkin*. The hope is to fall asleep long before you get to the end.

Cognitive shuffling hits a "sweet spot" between conscious and unconscious thought—enough of a task to keep unwelcome thoughts at bay, but not complicated enough to warrant much executive function. "All the while you're doing it, you're not thinking as much about your mortgage," Beaudoin says. 

However, Beaudoin emphasises that no cognitive treatment should be used in isolation. Good sleep hygiene and other science-backed recommendations, such as avoiding caffeine before bed, remain essential.
